Why Would Anyone Pay $200,000 for Someone Else’s Jacket?

The latest from Hollywood on the Potomac.

When I received a press release announcing Propstore’s upcoming $10 million Entertainment Memorabilia Auction, I had the same reaction many people probably would.

John Travolta’s legendary white Saturday Night Fever suit is expected to bring as much as $400,000. Frank the Rabbit’s haunting costume from Donnie Darko could sell for $200,000. A mosquito trapped in amber from Jurassic Park carries a six-figure estimate (I love that one), while Michael Keaton’s Batman Returns Batsuit, Johnny Depp’s Edward Scissorhand’s blades, the original Golden Snitch from Harry Potter and the Sorcerer’s Stone, Robert Downey Jr.’s illuminated Iron Man armor, Jack Nicholson’s stunt axe from The Shining, and even Michael Jackson’s prosthetic “Thriller” teeth are all headed to the auction block.

The Shinning

My first thought wasn’t, “Which one would I buy?” It was, “Why would anyone spend that kind of money on something someone else wore?”

So I asked someone who watches these bidding wars unfold every day.

Chuck Costas, Vice President of Business Development for Propstore in Los Angeles, says the answer has far less to do with clothing than with emotion.

“Collecting entertainment memorabilia is a way for someone to get closer to a movie or television show they love,“ Costas explains. “Looking at a costume or prop every day helps bring back some of that joy they felt when they first watched it.“

In other words, collectors aren’t buying fabric. They’re buying memories.

Batman Returns and Edward Scissorhand

That philosophy is reflected throughout this year’s four-day auction, which takes place August 26-29 in Los Angeles and features more than 1,700 original film and television props, costumes, production materials, autographed treasures and collectibles, carrying a combined pre-sale estimate of $10 million.

The undisputed headliner is John Travolta’s instantly recognizable white three-piece suit from Saturday Night Fever, worn during the film’s unforgettable disco competition and featured in many of its best-known promotional images. The costume also has its own Hollywood pedigree—it was later owned by legendary film critic Gene Siskel and even bears a handwritten inscription from Travolta himself.

Saturday Night Fever

Other marquee offerings include Frank the Rabbit’s principal costume from Donnie Darko, making its first public auction appearance after being exhibited at the Academy Museum of Motion Pictures; the screen-matched mosquito-in-amber fossil that launched the story in Steven Spielberg’s Jurassic Park; and one of the first six Golden Snitches created for Harry Potter and the Sorcerer’s Stone, used on set by the cast and crew as a lighting and scale reference.

According to Costas, most buyers aren’t museums or Wall Street investors.

“Overall, I’d say our main buyers are collectors who are fans of certain movies or television series,“ he says.

That emotional connection often translates into astonishing prices.

Jurassic Park

So what separates a $5,000 costume from one worth half a million dollars?

Several factors come into play, beginning with the popularity of the film itself. Franchises such as Star Wars, Indiana Jones, Back to the Future, Jaws, Terminator, and Aliens continue to command extraordinary prices because generations of fans want to own a tangible piece of cinematic history.

The importance of the item matters just as much.

As Costas puts it, “A collector would much rather own Batman’s Batarang rather than a Gotham City license plate because it’s a singular piece that the main character uses in key action sequences.“

Scarcity also drives value. While movie studios often produce multiple versions of costumes and props for filming, some iconic pieces survive in only one or two known examples. Add impeccable provenance and proof that the item actually appeared on screen, and collectors are willing to compete fiercely.

Harry Potter

Those same principles explain why this year’s auction spans such a wide range of coveted pieces. Whether it’s Johnny Depp’s original Edward Scissorhand’s blades, Michael Keaton’s Batman Returns Batsuit, Milla Jovovich’s illuminated Multi Pass from The Fifth Element, Robert Downey Jr.’s Iron Man upper body armor, or Jack Nicholson’s stunt axe from The Shining, each represents an unforgettable moment in movie history. Collectors aren’t simply buying an object—they’re buying a piece of the story.

Today’s technology has changed authentication as well.

“Collectors almost always prefer a piece that can be proven to have appeared on screen,“ Costas says. “With 4K technology, there have been many advances that allow one to see specific flaws or details on screen that match an item, which can definitively prove that it was used in the making of the film or television show.“

That level of verification is increasingly important in a market where fakes still circulate.

Frank the Rabbit

Propstore regularly rejects consignments that don’t pass scrutiny.

“We regularly reject items that we don’t think are correct or don’t check out. Doing the research is an important part of this business as we stand behind everything we sell,“ Costas says.

Beyond nostalgia, celebrity memorabilia has quietly become an investment category.

Costas points to one striking example: Jean-Luc Picard’s famous Ressikan Flute from Star Trek: The Next Generation. The prop sold for $237,500 in 2021. Just four years later, the very same piece brought $403,200.

Not everything appreciates, he cautions, but the overall market has grown dramatically over the past decade.

Star Trek

The biggest surprises often come when bidders ignore the estimates altogether.

This year, Propstore expected the memorable “Birthing Rhino” prop from Ace Ventura: When Nature Calls to sell for between $4,000 and $8,000.

Instead? Nearly $60,000. “Ultimately, it sold for 15 times the low estimate,“ Costas recalls.

Even after years in the business, auction day can still be unpredictable.

Some of the most dramatic moments happen when millions of dollars are on the line. Costas remembers a 2022 auction for a screen-matched Star Wars X-Wing miniature that ultimately sold for $2.375 million.

“The bidding continued for 15 to 20 minutes with tension after every bid at those high amounts,“ he says. “In the end, my client didn’t win.”

The tastes of collectors also evolve with each generation.

Golden Age legends like Charlie Chaplin and John Wayne no longer command the same attention they once did. Today’s buyers grew up with different heroes.

“It wouldn’t be unexpected to see Will Ferrell’s Elf costume or Adam Sandler’s Happy Gilmore jersey sell for more than costumes from Clark Gable or John Wayne,“ Costas says.

The Fifth

It’s nostalgia with a changing cast.

Looking ahead, Costas predicts artificial intelligence will help create more comprehensive databases tracking auction history and pricing, while fractional ownership could eventually allow multiple collectors to own shares in ultra-expensive artifacts—perhaps even something as iconic as Darth Vader’s lightsaber.

Brandon Alinger, Propstore’s Chief Operating Officer, believes these auctions are about more than collectibles.

Thriller

“At Propstore, we’re passionate about preserving and sharing the history of film and television through the original objects that helped bring these stories to life,” Alinger says. “From John Travolta’s iconic white suit from Saturday Night Fever and the amber fossil from Jurassic Park to a remarkable collection of autographed treasures, this auction reflects the incredible diversity of entertainment memorabilia and offers collectors the opportunity to own authentic pieces of the films, performers, and moments that continue to inspire audiences around the world.”

As for me?

I still can’t imagine paying $400,000 for John Travolta’s white disco suit—or six figures for a fake mosquito trapped in amber.

But after talking with Chuck Costas, I finally understand what collectors are really buying. They’re not purchasing costumes. They’re purchasing a ticket back to the first time they fell in love with a movie.

And for some fans, that’s priceless.
